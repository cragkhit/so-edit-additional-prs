\section{Full-Scope Before--After Robustness Check}
\label{sec:before-after-full}

Section~\ref{sec:before-after-robustness} reported a paired static-analysis comparison restricted to the 125 unique before--after histories whose \emph{Final Manual Validation} value is \texttt{Agress Yes}.
That scope answers the question \emph{among recommendations judged useful, how do quality metrics change?}
It cannot, by construction, ask whether the measured change differs between recommendations that were judged useful and those that were not, because every history it contains was judged useful.

This section reports the same three-tool analysis extended to \textbf{all 205 unique, dataset-eligible before--after histories} derivable from the complete 793-row Matcha manual-validation study, irrespective of the validation outcome.
The extraction pipeline, wrapper strategies (raw source, class-member, method-body), common-parse-mode requirement, statistical tests, and multiple-comparison correction are identical to those described in Section~\ref{sec:before-after-robustness}; only the inclusion filter differs.

Because the full scope admits recommendations judged useful, judged not useful, and judged inconsistently across different GitHub matches, every history carries an additional \emph{validation group} label: \texttt{ALL\_ACCEPTED} (judged useful in every GitHub match in which it appeared), \texttt{ALL\_REJECTED} (judged not useful in every match), and \texttt{MIXED} (judged useful in some matches and not in others).
This label supports a validity check the scoped analysis structurally could not perform: \emph{if the manual validation is measuring something real, the static-analysis change should differ between accepted and rejected recommendations.}
The \texttt{ALL\_REJECTED} group functions as a built-in negative control.

Two features distinguish this table from its scoped counterpart (Table~\ref{tab:ba-javaparser}).
First, code-line count moves from $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.273$ at $n=80$ to $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.070$ at $n=128$ (raw $p=0.004$), a near-miss rather than a clear null.
Six additional metrics -- method count, parameter count, local-variable count, the cyclomatic-complexity proxy, abrupt-exit count, and exception-handling count -- now cross the \emph{uncorrected} $p<0.05$ threshold, versus two at the scoped size; none survives Holm correction (all $p_{\text{Holm}}\geq0.18$).
Second, every rank-biserial correlation in the table is non-negative: at full scope, revisions skew toward \emph{increasing} every structural count and toward decreasing none, in contrast to the mixed positive/negative pattern at $n=80$.
Taken together, these are consistent with a real but modestly-sized ``revisions add code'' effect that the smaller sample was underpowered to resolve, rather than with the absence of an effect.

The caveat raised for the scoped analysis still applies to individual rows: several of the largest rank-biserial values (loop count $+0.82$, try-with-resources $+1.00$, try count $+0.62$) rest on 2--12 nonzero-delta pairs out of 128, and are symptoms of a tiny effective sample for those metrics rather than evidence of large competing effects.

\subsubsection*{By recommendation type (uncorrected, exploratory)}

Within the Bug Fixing subgroup ($n=12$), code-line count shows a raw $p=0.039$ (7/12 pairs increased, median $\Delta=+1$); within Improving Code ($n=65$), $p=0.099$ (33/65 increased, median $\Delta=+1$).
Both figures are essentially unchanged from the scoped analysis, because the same \texttt{Agress Yes} histories dominate these two subgroups at either scope.
As at the scoped size, every other structural metric in both subgroups is dominated by unchanged pairs.

\subsubsection*{PMD subgroups}

Both subgroup dimensions are non-significant throughout ($p\geq0.47$), with most pairs recording zero violations on both sides.
By recommendation type, the bug-risk dimension gives 0 improved / 10 unchanged / 1 worsened for Bug Fixing ($n=11$, $p=1.000$) and 5 / 55 / 3 for Improving Code ($n=63$, $p=0.844$).

None is significant, but the ordering is the same gradient JavaParser and PMD show.

\subsection{Cross-tool synthesis}
\label{sec:ba-full-synthesis}

Two findings distinguish the full scope from the 125-pair scope.

First, \textbf{the additional power reveals a clearer and more internally consistent JavaParser signal}.
Code-line count moves from $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.273$ ($n=80$) to $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.070$ ($n=128$), and all 19 structural metrics now show a non-negative rank-biserial correlation, against a mixed-sign pattern at the smaller scope.
This supports the ``underpowered, not necessarily null'' reading that Section~\ref{sec:before-after-synthesis} already flagged, while still falling short of $\alpha=0.05$ after correction.

Second, \textbf{all three tools independently show the same validation-outcome gradient}: \texttt{ALL\_ACCEPTED} histories carry a stronger improvement or growth signal than \texttt{ALL\_REJECTED}, with \texttt{MIXED} weakest or noisiest.
JavaParser shows this most clearly and produces the only individually notable result in the study (code-line count, \texttt{ALL\_ACCEPTED}, uncorrected $p=0.008$); PMD and Checkstyle reproduce the direction with far less power.
Convergence of three independently implemented tools on one direction is a modestly encouraging signal, but it is not the same evidentiary bar as three tools independently reaching significance, and we describe it accordingly.

Neither finding overturns the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:before-after-robustness}: \textbf{no metric survives Holm correction in the primary full-sample test, in any tool, at either scope}.
What the larger sample adds is a clearer picture of \emph{where} the available signal is concentrated --- in code growth, and specifically among accepted recommendations --- and \emph{why} the smaller scope's null results are more consistent with limited power than with a genuine absence of effect.

\subsection{Implications}
\label{sec:ba-full-implications}

The \texttt{ALL\_ACCEPTED} versus \texttt{ALL\_REJECTED} gradient is the most useful new evidence this expanded scope produces, because it answers a validity question a reviewer could plausibly ask directly: if the manual validation measures something real, quality metrics should differ between accepted and rejected recommendations.
They do, in the expected direction, across all three tools, for the one metric with an estimable effect size.
This is worth reporting explicitly, with the caveat that only the JavaParser result is individually notable ($p=0.008$, uncorrected) and that none of the validation-group comparisons is Holm-corrected.

The full-scope numbers should not be presented as replacing the 125-pair analysis.
The two scopes answer different questions --- ``among recommendations judged useful, how do quality metrics change?'' versus ``across everything Matcha recommended, does the measured change track the validation judgment?'' --- and both belong in the manuscript, doing different jobs.
Likewise, code-line count at $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.070$ should be flagged as a near-miss: neither rounded up to ``significant'' nor dismissed as ``not significant'' without qualification.
Had code-line count been pre-registered as \emph{the} primary outcome, it would face a far less severe correction and could plausibly cross $\alpha=0.05$ at this scope.

Two limits should be stated plainly rather than worked around.
PMD remains too sparse to support strong claims at either scope: 118 violations across 124 pairs leaves no subgroup analysis informative, and single-digit rule transitions should not be over-interpreted.
And while $n=124$--$129$ improves on $n=77$--$81$, it is still modest, particularly once split into three validation groups.

Accordingly, we revise the claim in the same direction as Section~\ref{sec:before-after-implications}, with one addition: compared with their original versions, recent Stack Overflow revisions show small, non-significant, and rule-dependent changes in static-analysis indicators of maintainability, bug-risk, and readability; the clearest measurable tendency is toward \emph{adding} code, which is concentrated among recommendations independently judged useful; and the strongest evidence for improvement continues to come from manual classification and pull-request acceptance rather than from paired static-analysis metrics alone.

\subsection{Threats and limitations specific to this scope}
\label{sec:ba-full-limitations}

All figures above are read directly from the CSV/JSON outputs of the three full-scope notebooks; no new statistical test was run to produce this section.

At full scope, \texttt{recommendation\_group} includes two categories excluded from the recommendation-type subgroup tests: \texttt{NO\_TYPE} (histories with no accepted study row to classify under a Baltes category --- 48 of 128 JavaParser-analyzed histories) and the pre-existing \texttt{MULTIPLE\_TYPES} (3 analyzed histories).
The latter was excluded in the scoped analysis on the same grounds.

Every \texttt{validation\_group} $p$-value reported here is uncorrected, both within a tool's three-group comparison and across the three tools that each ran it; all should be read as exploratory.

PMD's recommendation-type subgroups contain 11 Bug Fixing histories where JavaParser and Checkstyle each contain 12, because PMD's parse-mode coverage excludes one additional Bug Fixing history.
This is an ordinary consequence of each tool selecting its own common parse mode independently, not a data inconsistency.

Finally, this section does not include the SpotBugs sensitivity analysis or the manual audit of analyzer findings; neither is yet present under \texttt{results/}.
